Orientation · Week 1 of 19

What Is the Bible?

The Bible isn't one book but a small library — 39 Old Testament books (the Hebrew TaNaK: Torah, Prophets, Writings) written in Hebrew and Aramaic over more than 1,000 years, and 27 New Testament books written in Greek within a few decades of Jesus by his early followers. Different Christian traditions include a few additional books beyond this core, but all agree the story centers on Jesus.

Listen The Bible as Divine Literary Art What's in Your Bible?
Discussion guide (10 questions)
  1. When you hear the word "Bible," what comes to mind first — a single book, a rulebook, a library, a story? Where do you think that picture came from?
  2. Read 2 Timothy 3:15-17. What does Paul call Scripture, and what does he say it's useful for? His word for "God-breathed" is the Greek theopneustos — what does it mean for something written by human authors, shaped by their own time and place, to also be described this way?
  3. The Old and New Testaments were written by dozens of authors across more than a thousand years, in different languages, under very different circumstances. Why do you think God chose to reveal himself this way, rather than through one text handed down all at once?
  4. Read Hebrews 4:12. What does this verse say God's word actually does? The author calls it "living and active" rather than just "true and reliable" — is that how you typically experience the Bible when you open it, or does it feel more like a historical document or a moral rulebook? What would need to change for "living and active" to be your honest, default expectation?
  5. Read Luke 24:13-27. What does Jesus do for the two disciples on the road, and where does he start explaining himself from, according to verse 27? What does that tell you about how Jesus himself understood and read the Old Testament?
  6. The Bible mixes narrative, poetry, law, letters, and wisdom writing. How do you think these different kinds of writing work together to tell one story, instead of just being a grab-bag of unrelated material?
  7. Christian traditions (Protestant, Catholic, Orthodox) don't all agree on the exact list of books in the Bible. Does that surprise or unsettle you? Does it change how much confidence you place in Scripture — why or why not?
  8. BibleProject argues the Bible tells one continuous story that leads to Jesus. Does that story continue today, in the church and in your own life? If so, how do you see yourself as part of it?
  9. What's one assumption about the Bible — how it was written, what it's for, how it should be read — that you're willing to have challenged over these 19 weeks?
  10. As you start this study, what's one thing you're hoping to walk away with?

Orientation · Week 2 of 19

The Story of the Bible

The Bible reads like a library, but it's actually one continuous story with a plot that moves from creation to new creation. It opens with God shaping a good world and inviting humans to rule it well, and it closes with God and humanity dwelling together in a renewed creation. In between, the story tracks humanity's repeated failure to trust God's definition of good and evil, God's choice to work through one family — Israel — to bless the whole world, and finally Jesus, who fulfills Israel's calling and throws the story open to everyone.

Listen What Is the Story of the Bible?
Discussion guide (10 questions)
  1. Think about the stories you grew up with — fairy tales, family stories, the "story" your country tells about itself. What made those stories powerful or sticky? Do you tend to think of the Bible more like that kind of shaping story, or more like a reference book you consult for answers?
  2. Read Genesis 1:27-28. What does God tell the man and woman to do right after he creates them? What do you think it means to be made "in God's image," and how does that shape what you think humans are actually for?
  3. Read Genesis 3:6-7. What does the woman see in the fruit that makes her want it, and what do she and Adam actually do — and what happens to them right afterward? Why do you think the temptation to define good and evil on our own terms, rather than trusting God's, is still so tempting for us today?
  4. Read Genesis 3:15. What exactly does God say will happen between the serpent and the offspring of the woman? Why do you think God announces this hope so early, long before anyone could see how it would be fulfilled?
  5. Read Genesis 12:2-3. What exactly does God promise Abraham in these verses? Why start small — one couple, one family — instead of fixing everything for the whole world all at once?
  6. Read Mark 1:14-15. What exactly does Jesus announce, and what two things does he call people to do in response? Given everything Israel had been waiting for, why is this such a loaded, pivotal announcement?
  7. Read Matthew 28:5-6. What does the angel actually tell the women at the tomb? What do you make of the claim that Jesus' resurrection didn't just reverse one death, but broke the power of evil and death themselves?
  8. Read Romans 3:21-26. What does Paul say this righteousness from God is based on, and who does he say it's available to? How does this connect back to God's original promise to Abraham that all nations would be blessed through him?
  9. Read Revelation 21:1-4. What does this passage say God will do, and what does it say will be gone for good? How does knowing where the story ends change the way you live in the middle of it, right now?
  10. Now that you've seen the whole arc in one sitting, what's one book or story in the Bible you're curious to go back and reread with this bigger picture in mind?

Literary Styles in the Bible

The Bible isn't written in one uniform style — tonight we'll look at a few of the different kinds of writing it uses, and what changes once you notice which one you're in.

Watch the video Download PDF
Listen Literary Genres and the Stories We Tell Ourselves Poetry, Narrative, and Prose Discourse
Discussion guide (10 questions)
  1. When you picture "the Bible" in your mind, do you picture more story, more poetry, or more of a rulebook or instruction manual? Where do you think that impression came from?
  2. Narrative makes up nearly half of the Bible. Why do you think God chose to reveal so much of himself through story, rather than through straightforward teaching or a list of rules?
  3. Read Jonah 1. This chapter is narrative — a character (Jonah), a setting (a ship fleeing to Tarshish), and a mounting conflict (the storm). Who does most of the chapter's action actually center on after verse 3, and how do they respond to what's happening?
  4. Jonah is running from God's call to preach to Nineveh, a city Israel hated. By the end of the chapter, the pagan sailors on the ship end up looking more godly than Jonah does. What do you think the author wants us to notice by setting up that contrast?
  5. Read Jonah 2. What does Jonah actually say in this prayer — what does he acknowledge about what's happened to him, and what does he vow to do? Jonah's prayer from inside the fish shifts from narrative into poetry. Poetry works differently than narrative — it's meant to be felt and imagined, not just informed. What images or phrases stand out to you here, and how do they land differently than if this moment had just been described in plain narration?
  6. Jonah is about to storm off in anger at God again just two chapters later, after Nineveh repents. Does his prayer in chapter 2 sound to you like genuine repentance, or something else? What do you make of that tension?
  7. Read Romans 8:1-4 and 8:31-39. What does Paul say is now true for those who are in Christ Jesus, and what does he list that can't separate us from the love of God? This is prose discourse — Paul building a connected, logical argument (notice words like "therefore," "because," "so that"). What is Paul actually trying to persuade his readers to believe or do in this chapter?
  8. Discourse like Romans 8 is built to engage your reasoning; poetry like Jonah's prayer is built to engage your imagination and emotions. Which style do you personally connect with more easily when you read Scripture, and which one do you tend to skim past?
  9. The same Bible contains a fish-swallows-a-prophet story and a dense theological argument about no condemnation and nothing separating us from God's love. How do you think these very different kinds of writing work together, rather than feeling like a mismatched grab bag?
  10. If you started paying closer attention to what kind of writing you're reading — story, poem, or argument — what do you think would change about how you read Scripture, or what you'd expect to get out of it?

Narrative · Week 4 of 19

The Bible as Jewish Meditation Literature

Ancient Hebrew narrative works differently than most of the storytelling we're used to today. Where modern stories spell out what characters look like, why they act the way they do, and how we're supposed to feel about it, biblical authors frequently leave those details out on purpose. These gaps are invitations to slow down, sit with the ambiguity, and return to the same story again and again over a lifetime. The Bible itself describes this kind of slow, repeated engagement as "meditation."

Watch the video Download PDF
Listen Why Isn't There More Detail in Bible Stories? The Bible as Jewish Meditation Literature
Discussion guide (9 questions)
  1. Think of a book, film, or song you've come back to more than once and noticed something new each time. What was it, and what did repeated encounters bring out that you missed the first time around?
  2. Biblical narrative often leaves out details modern readers expect — what people looked like, why they made the choices they made. Does that surprise you? Have you ever felt frustrated reading a Bible story because information seemed to be missing?
  3. Take the story of Cain and Abel as an example: we're never told why God had regard for Abel's offering but not Cain's. Why might a storyteller want you to feel confused on purpose?
  4. When an author leaves ambiguity in a story, different readers can honestly land on different interpretations of it. What's the risk in that kind of open-ended storytelling, and what's the potential reward?
  5. Read Psalm 1. What does Psalm 1 say the "blessed" person avoids, and what does he do instead? The Hebrew word translated "meditate" is used elsewhere to describe a bear growling low over its prey, or a dove cooing — a quiet, absorbed sound, not an empty, silent mind. How is that picture different from what you'd typically picture when you hear the word "meditation"?
  6. What would it actually look like to build a habit of delighting in and meditating on Scripture "day and night" into an ordinary week?
  7. Psalm 1 compares this kind of person to "a tree planted by streams of water," steadily bearing fruit. Why do you think patient, repeated engagement with Scripture gets compared to a slow-growing tree instead of something quicker, like a sudden flash of insight?
  8. If success in reading the Bible isn't about extracting a tidy takeaway every single time, but about learning to sit with ambiguity and let a passage shape you over years, how does that change what a good "quiet time" or devotional habit might actually look like?
  9. What's one story or passage you've read many times but never really slowed down to sit with? What would it look like to "meditate" on it, in the Psalm 1 sense, sometime this week?

Narrative · Week 5 of 19

Plot in Biblical Narrative

Nearly half of the Bible is written as narrative, and narrative works by arranging characters and events into a plot — a conflict that builds toward a climax and finds resolution. Biblical stories almost always work on multiple layers at once: a small scene fits inside a larger story, which fits inside a bigger movement, which fits inside the single sweeping storyline of the whole Bible. Reading a scene in isolation, without asking how it fits into that larger plot, can completely flip its meaning.

Listen Plot in Biblical Narrative
Discussion guide (10 questions)
  1. Have you ever badly misread a movie or book because you only caught one scene out of context — like walking into a film halfway through? What happened once you finally saw the whole thing?
  2. The video walked through the whole Gideon story in Judges 6-7. Before we go further: in that story, where do you think the real conflict actually sits? Not "how will Gideon win the battle," but what tension does the narrator seem most interested in — and where does it get resolved, if it does?
  3. Read Judges 8:22-31. After the famous victory, Gideon gives the theologically correct answer — "I will not rule over you… the LORD will rule over you" (v. 23) — and then immediately makes a gold ephod that becomes "a snare to Gideon and to his household," and names a son Abimelech, which means "my father is king." What has happened to Gideon by the end of his own story? Does his ending change how you read the victory that came before it?
  4. Some readers sort biblical plots into a "comedy" shape (a flawed character is humbled and then rises) or a "tragedy" shape (a character with every advantage slowly self-destructs). Looking at the whole span of Judges 6-8, which shape fits Gideon best — and what's your evidence?
  5. Read Jonah 3. (Quick recap: Jonah has run from God's call to preach against Nineveh, been thrown overboard and swallowed by a great fish, and been given a second chance.) Imagine the book ended right here — as more than one children's Bible actually does. What would the story be about, and what would you take away from it?
  6. Read Jonah 4. What does this final chapter reveal the book was really about all along? Skim back over chapter 1 — how does Jonah's behavior there read differently now?
  7. Judges ends by repeating, four times, "In those days there was no king in Israel" — twice adding, "everyone did what was right in his own eyes." If Gideon is one of the better judges, what is the book as a whole building toward? How does that larger arc change what the Gideon story is "about"?
  8. Read 1 Samuel 8:4-9. The book of Judges seems to be reaching for a king — but when Israel finally demands one, God's answer to Samuel is "they have rejected me from being king over them," and he grants the request anyway. The kings that follow mostly fail, David included. So the thing Judges wants turns out to be part of the problem, not the fix. Pulling back to the whole Bible: what kind of leadership does the story actually turn out to need?
  9. This week's podcast compares misreading a story to hiking toward what looks like the summit — then realizing it was a false peak on a much bigger climb. Has that happened in your own life: something you read at the time as the end of the story — a peak, or a defeat — that turned out to be the middle of something larger?
  10. Think of a familiar Bible story you've mostly encountered in bite-sized pieces — a sermon point, a devotional, a kids' Bible version. What might change if you read the whole surrounding narrative in one sitting instead?

Narrative · Week 6 of 19

Character in Biblical Narrative

Biblical authors build characters very differently than a modern novel or movie would. Instead of describing what someone looks like or explaining their inner thoughts, the Bible tends to reveal character through a handful of carefully chosen details — a name, a physical trait, an action, a line of dialogue — and then steps back and lets you decide what to make of it. That's a strategic choice, not a lack of skill: the author is trusting you to do some of the work yourself.

Listen Character in Biblical Narrative
Discussion guide (9 questions)
  1. Think of a character from a movie or show you love who isn't simply "good" or "bad" — someone morally complicated, hard to pin down. What keeps you interested in them?
  2. Read 1 Samuel 9:1-2. What details does the text give us about Saul when he's introduced — his family background, his appearance? Why do you think the narrator chose to tell us this detail about him instead of simply saying he'd make a good king or a bad one?
  3. Read Genesis 12:10-20. What does Abraham ask Sarah to tell the Egyptians, and why does he ask her to do it? The narrator never says whether this was right or wrong — he just tells us what happened and what it led to. What does it do to you as a reader when the Bible refuses to hand you a verdict on a character like this?
  4. Read Genesis 32:28. What new name is Jacob given here, and what reason does the text give for it? Names carry a lot of weight in biblical narrative — they're small brushstrokes with big implications. What does it tell you that God renames a lifelong deceiver "one who struggles with God," rather than something like "one who has arrived"?
  5. Read Daniel 6. What do Daniel's enemies admit they can't find against him, and what's the one thing they're able to use? How does a character like Daniel function differently in a story than someone like Abraham or Jacob?
  6. Biblical characters are almost always a mixed bag — capable of real faith and real failure, sometimes in the very same chapter. Why do you think the biblical authors were so committed to portraying people this way instead of giving us clear heroes and villains?
  7. God is the one character who shows up on the very first page of the Bible and is still present on the last page. What difference does it make that the Bible's real "main character," in that sense, isn't any human being but God himself?
  8. Which biblical character's flaws or failures do you find yourself most relating to right now, and why?
  9. Given everything we've talked about tonight, how might you read Bible stories differently this week — paying more attention to what's shown through a character's choices than what's simply told to you about them?

Narrative · Week 7 of 19

Setting in Biblical Narrative

In storytelling, the place and time where a scene happens isn't just backdrop — it carries meaning and sets expectations, the same way a courtroom or a haunted house immediately cues us in on what kind of story we're about to watch. Biblical authors use setting the same way: certain places and time periods, like "the east" or the number forty, pick up meaning across the whole Bible, so that every time they show up again, they arrive loaded with memory and anticipation.

Listen Setting in Biblical Narrative
Discussion guide (9 questions)
  1. Think of a movie or show where the setting alone told you what kind of story you were about to watch — a dark old house, a courtroom, a small town. What settings instantly set your expectations like that?
  2. Read Genesis 3:23-24, Genesis 4:16, and Genesis 11:1-2. Which direction is each person or group heading? What has just happened in each story at the moment they go that way? Then read 2 Chronicles 36:15-20 — where does the whole nation eventually end up? Once a narrator has done this a few times, what does the mere mention of someone moving "east" set you up to expect?
  3. Read Genesis 7:12. How long do the rains last? Now skim two other "forties": Moses on the mountain while the people wait below (Exodus 24:18; 32:1), and the spies' report and what follows (Numbers 13:25; 14:33-34). What kind of situation does "forty" keep marking? How do people tend to do during it?
  4. Read Matthew 4:1-11. Jesus gets the same forty-day setup, in the same wilderness. How do his forty days go differently from Israel's? Why might a Gospel writer want that echo right at the start of Jesus' ministry?
  5. Read Matthew 2:13-23. In the Old Testament, Egypt is the place of bondage: Abraham's family first goes down there to escape a famine, their descendants end up enslaved for generations, and even after the exodus the law warns Israel never to go back (Deuteronomy 17:16). "Down to Egypt" is a move you've learned to read as trouble. Yet when Jesus' family flees Herod, Egypt becomes the place of safety instead. Why would an author deliberately flip a setting on us like this?
  6. Read Genesis 19:30-38, then Numbers 25:1-3, then Deuteronomy 23:3. By the time you've read those three, what does the name "Moab" make you brace for? Now read Ruth 1:1-5 — a family from Bethlehem moves to Moab, and the sons marry Moabite women. What do you expect this to lead to?
  7. Read Ruth 4:13-17. Whose great-grandmother does Ruth the Moabite turn out to be? What is the author doing by placing the origin of that line in Moab, of all places — the setting the story has trained us to dread? How is this the same move Matthew makes with Egypt?
  8. Is there a "setting" in your own life right now — a season, a place, a kind of waiting — that feels like your own wilderness or your own "forty days"? What do you think might be going on in that timing?
  9. As we close, what's one place or time-marker in Scripture that you now want to pay closer attention to the next time you come across it?

Narrative · Week 8 of 19

Design Patterns in Biblical Narrative

Biblical authors weave individual stories together into one larger story using deliberate patterns — repeated words, repeated scenes, and repeated sequences of action that echo across hundreds of years and dozens of different characters. Once you start noticing these patterns, you realize that stories placed far apart in the Bible are actually in conversation with one another, and that they're all building toward Jesus.

Listen Live from Milpitas, Pt. 1 Live from Milpitas, Pt. 2 Crossing the Chaotic Waters Chaotic Waters and Baptism
Discussion guide (9 questions)
  1. Have you ever noticed a movie or show deliberately echo an earlier scene — the same shot, the same line, the same choice — to make a point by comparison? What did that repetition make you think or feel?
  2. The video traced one pattern — see it, want it, take it — from Eve through Aaron's golden calf to David. Read Genesis 4:1-16, the very next story after Eden. What does God say to Cain in verses 9-12 that he has already said, almost word for word, in Genesis 3? What is the author signaling by making the second story rhyme this closely with the first?
  3. Read Joshua 7:1, then 7:19-26. Centuries later, an Israelite named Achan is caught. Read his confession in verse 21 — "I saw… I coveted… I took… hidden in my tent." How many earlier stories is that one sentence built to echo? What does it change about a story to realize it was written to rhyme with others?
  4. This pattern turns up with completely different people — a couple in a garden, a priest at a mountain, a king on a roof, a soldier with stolen loot — spread across more than a thousand years. And the serpent from Genesis 3 never appears again. What are the biblical authors saying about human nature by building the same shape into story after story?
  5. Read Luke 3:21-22 and 4:1-13 (and glance at the last line of the genealogy in between, 3:38). Here's a different kind of design pattern — one author, one repeated phrase. Luke lines up scene after scene that each turn on "Son of God": the voice at the baptism, the genealogy ending "…the son of Adam, the son of God," then Satan's "if you are the Son of God." What is Luke getting you to do by circling the same title from several angles instead of just stating it once?
  6. In Gethsemane, facing his own great temptation, Jesus says "not my will, but yours be done" — the first time in the whole story someone meets that moment and doesn't take. You've seen this from the video. Push further: why does it matter that the pattern finally breaks through surrender and suffering rather than through Jesus getting what he wanted?
  7. Once you're looking for patterns, it gets tempting to "find" them everywhere — Tim Mackie admits this is the real risk of reading the Bible this way. What do you think separates a connection the author built on purpose from one a reader imagined?
  8. Where do you see the "see it, want it, take it" pattern running in your own life, or in the temptations you face most often?
  9. Knowing how deliberately these patterns were woven across dozens of books and centuries, how does that shape the way you think about the Bible — as one coordinated story rather than a loose anthology?

Gospel · Week 9 of 19

The Gospel

The word "gospel" means "good news," and in the New Testament it specifically means the announcement that God's kingdom has arrived in our world through the life, death, and resurrection of Jesus. Matthew, Mark, Luke, and John each tell this same essential story, but each author arranges and highlights different details to bring out a different facet of who Jesus is.

Discussion guide (9 questions)
  1. What's a piece of genuinely good news you've received in your life that changed how you saw everything else going on around you at the time?
  2. Read John 20:31. According to this verse, why does John say he wrote these things down? What does it mean to you that the Gospel writers weren't just trying to record history, but were trying to persuade you to actually trust and follow Jesus?
  3. Read Luke 2:1-21. Who is the news of Jesus' birth announced to first, and how does the angel describe it? Why do you think this news is first delivered to shepherds rather than to religious or political leaders?
  4. Read Luke 4:16-21. In his hometown synagogue, Jesus reads a passage from Isaiah and then says, "Today this Scripture is fulfilled in your hearing." What does the Isaiah passage say he's been sent to do — and who is it for? What does that tell you about what the "kingdom of God" actually looks like when it shows up?
  5. Read Mark 15:21-47. Who's present at the cross in this passage, and what does the centurion say when Jesus dies? Mark spends a huge portion of his short Gospel on the last week of Jesus' life, and this passage is a big part of why. Why do you think the Gospel writers treat the crucifixion, of all things, as the climax of the entire story?
  6. Read Mark 16:1-8. What do the women find when they arrive at the tomb, and how does the text say they respond? Why might Mark choose to end his Gospel on a note of alarm, trembling, and silence instead of a more confident, victorious one?
  7. Skim the opening of each Gospel — Matthew 1:1-17, Mark 1:1-8, Luke 1:1-4, John 1:1-14. Four writers, one subject, four completely different starting points: a genealogy, a wilderness preacher, a formal preface, the beginning of everything. What does each author's choice of where to begin tell you about what he most wants you to see in Jesus?
  8. The Gospel writers rarely tell you outright how to feel about Jesus — instead they show you how other people in the story respond to him and let you make up your own mind. Where have you seen your own response to Jesus mirrored in one of the characters we read about this week?
  9. As you think about the whole message of the gospel — that God's kingdom has broken into the world through Jesus — what's one part of your own life where you need to let that news actually change something this week?

Gospel · Week 10 of 19

Parables

Jesus was a master storyteller, and many of his most famous teachings come to us as short, vivid stories called parables. They aren't simple moral fables meant to illustrate a lesson — they're how Jesus announced that God's kingdom was breaking into the world, often in ways that confused, provoked, or upended his listeners' expectations. Reading a parable well means asking who's listening, what assumption is being challenged, and what decision the story is pushing you toward.

Discussion guide (9 questions)
  1. Think of a time someone made a point to you through a story instead of just telling you directly — a parent, a friend, a movie. Why do you think they told a story instead of just saying it plainly? Did it land differently?
  2. Read Luke 10:25-37. In the story itself, who passes by the wounded man without stopping, and who actually helps him (vv. 31-33)? Most people summarize this as a story about being a good person who helps those in need. But look closely at who's asking Jesus the question, and why (v. 25, 29). What is Jesus actually confronting by making a despised Samaritan the hero and the respected religious leaders the ones who fail?
  3. Jesus' parables often refuse to hand listeners a tidy moral — instead they leave the audience to draw their own conclusion, the way Nathan's story about a stolen lamb forced King David to convict himself before he realized the story was about him. Why might indirect, unfinished stories change people more effectively than direct instructions or lectures?
  4. Read Matthew 18:25-35. What does the master do for the first servant (v. 27), and what does that same servant then do to a fellow servant who owes him money (vv. 28-30)? Where do you see this same pattern show up in your own life or relationships — having received grace, but being slow to extend it to someone else?
  5. Many of Jesus' parables feature an authority figure (a king, master, father, or landowner) alongside two very different responses from those under him. Why do you think Jesus kept returning to this setup — one authority, two contrasting responses — when he talked about God's kingdom?
  6. Read Luke 15. What does each owner do the moment the lost thing is found — the shepherd with his sheep, the woman with her coin, the father with his son (vv. 5-6, 9, 20-24)? Jesus tells these three connected stories all as a direct response to religious leaders grumbling that he was welcoming "sinners" to his table (Luke 15:1-2). Which of the three images (a wandering sheep, a lost coin, a runaway son) feels closest to your own story of being sought out or welcomed back? Why?
  7. In the parable of the two sons, the father runs to embrace the younger son before he can finish his rehearsed apology, and then goes out a second time to plead with the resentful older brother standing outside the party. What does this suggest about how God pursues both the obviously lost and the quietly resentful?
  8. Jesus deliberately taught in a way that revealed truth to those willing to wrestle with it while leaving it puzzling to those who weren't. Why might God prefer that kind of teaching over simply stating things as clearly and directly as possible?
  9. Of the three passages we read tonight, which parable feels like it's aimed most directly at you this season — the story of the Samaritan, the unforgiving servant, or the lost sheep, coin, and son? What's one thing you sense it's inviting you to reconsider or change?

Poetry · Week 11 of 19

The Art of Biblical Poetry

Roughly a third of the Bible is poetry, and it works differently than narrative. Instead of moving a plot forward, biblical poetry slows down and lingers, inviting readers into an experience rather than just handing over information. Its basic building block is the couplet — two short lines placed side by side, where the second line completes, deepens, or contrasts the first — which is part of why poetry became the primary way the Hebrew prophets recorded God's own speech.

Discussion guide (9 questions)
  1. Do you gravitate toward poetry, or does it usually go over your head? Is there a song lyric, quote, or line of poetry that has stuck with you over the years? What makes it memorable?
  2. Read Psalm 29. How many times does the phrase "the voice of the LORD" turn up? What is the poem claiming about that storm by calling the thunder God's "voice"?
  3. Look again at Psalm 29. The opening (vv. 1-2) says "Ascribe to the LORD" three times; a line like verse 5 says nearly the same thing twice ("The voice of the LORD breaks the cedars; the LORD breaks the cedars of Lebanon"). Biblical poetry is built on this doubling, and the second line almost never just repeats the first. Pick a couplet and work out what the second line adds, sharpens, or intensifies. What does that doubling do to the pace of your reading?
  4. Read Judges 4:17-22, then Judges 5:24-27. The death of Sisera is told twice — first as narrative, then, one chapter later, as poetry in the Song of Deborah. What does the poem let you feel, or linger on, that the plain account doesn't? Why might the Bible keep both versions?
  5. Read Psalm 51:1-12. David reaches for one image after another — a stain that needs washing, a record that needs blotting out, guilt "ever before" him, a heart that has to be created new. Why do you think one image wasn't enough for him? Which of these pictures for sin or forgiveness rings most true to your own experience?
  6. Read Psalm 8. What two things does this short poem set side by side — the vast night sky, and what else? How does holding both in one small poem land differently than a flat statement that "humans matter to God"?
  7. Poetry is terse by design — it says more by using fewer, more carefully chosen words rather than explaining everything outright. Why might God have chosen to reveal so much of who he is, and so much of what he asks of us, through compressed poetry instead of straightforward prose?
  8. If a third of the Bible is written to be slowed down and savored rather than read quickly for information, how does that change the way you typically approach your Bible reading, if at all?
  9. Pick one line from Psalm 29, the Song of Deborah, Psalm 51, or Psalm 8 that stood out to you tonight. What would it look like to actually sit with that single line this week instead of moving on to the next chapter?

Poetry · Week 12 of 19

Poetic Metaphor

Metaphor isn't just decoration in language — it's the basic way humans make sense of the world. Biblical poets draw from a shared set of images rooted in Israel's story: chaotic, stormy waters as danger and death; dry land and gardens as safety and life; wild animals as threat; marriage as the picture of covenant faithfulness. Because these images get reused across books written centuries apart, one picture can carry an entire web of meaning that stretches from Genesis all the way to Revelation.

Poetry · Week 13 of 19

The Book of Psalms

The book of Psalms is the largest collection of poetry in the Bible, but it isn't a random anthology — it was carefully arranged to be read from beginning to end, retelling the whole biblical story in poetic form. It opens with poems about God's instruction and God's coming king, works through centuries of lament tied to David's story and Israel's exile, and closes with an unrestrained five-poem burst of praise — functioning as a kind of portable temple people could carry with them wherever they were.

Prophecy · Week 14 of 19

The Prophets

The Old Testament prophets are often remembered as fortune-tellers, but that's not really what a "prophet" means in the Bible. A prophet was God's messenger — someone who had a radical encounter with God's presence and was then sent to speak on his behalf, mostly to call Israel back to faithfulness in the covenant relationship. Most of what fills these fifteen books isn't prediction at all — it's blunt accusation of injustice, urgent calls to repent, and some of the most hope-filled poetry in the whole Bible sitting right alongside the harshest warnings.

Wisdom · Week 15 of 19

The Books of Solomon

Three books in the Bible are connected to King Solomon: Proverbs, Ecclesiastes, and Song of Songs. Solomon asked God for wisdom instead of wealth or power, and for a while he ruled Israel in remarkable abundance — but he ultimately let his heart be pulled away by everything he had gathered. These three books pick up his story from different angles, each wrestling with the same question that goes all the way back to the garden of Eden: will we take hold of wisdom on our own terms, or receive it as a gift from God?

Apocalyptic · Week 16 of 19

Apocalyptic Literature

"Apocalypse" doesn't mean the end of the world — it's a Greek word that means "to uncover" or "reveal." In the Bible, an apocalypse happens when God pulls back the curtain on ordinary life and lets someone see things from a heavenly vantage point, transforming how they see their circumstances. Understanding it means learning to trace its imagery — the chaotic sea, wild beasts, a throne room in the heavens — through the whole story of the Bible, rather than treating it as a private code to crack.

Law · Week 17 of 19

Biblical Law

The first books of the Bible contain more than 600 laws, and it's easy to either skip past them or read them as a strict behavior manual. But in the story of the Bible, the law isn't a divine checklist handed down from nowhere — it's the terms of a covenant relationship, given to a people God had already rescued. Israel is called to be a "kingdom of priests," and the laws are meant to spell out what a nation living in relationship with God could actually look like.

Letters · Week 18 of 19

New Testament Letters: Historical Context

The New Testament letters weren't written as timeless essays on theology — they're real letters from a real sender to a real audience, written for a particular purpose in response to a particular situation. Reading them well means asking the same questions you'd ask about anyone else's mail: who's writing, who's receiving, what history do they share, and what circumstance prompted the letter in the first place. These letters also landed in the honor-and-status world of the Roman Empire — a value system the gospel kept turning upside down.

Letters · Week 19 of 19

New Testament Letters: Literary Context

Paul's letters weren't dashed off in one sitting — they were carefully composed over weeks, often with input from a team of co-workers, and built to be read aloud in one sitting to a gathered church. Ancient letters followed a predictable shape: an opening naming the sender and recipient, a thanksgiving, the main body of the argument, and a closing. Because these letters were meant to be heard as a single, coherent whole, their authors used repeated words and transition phrases to signal how one idea built on the next.
